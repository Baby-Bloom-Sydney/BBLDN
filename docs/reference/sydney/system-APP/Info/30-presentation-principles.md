# 30 — Presentation Principles

> How Baby Bloom should present anything. Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People* + Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* synthesised into operating principles, then reconciled with the aura discipline from [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md). Where the source frameworks recommend tactics the brand rejects, this doc separates the *principle* (kept) from the *tactic* (replaced).

---

## The two source frameworks

**Carnegie (1936)** is a manual on how to influence other people without making them feel manipulated. Its core insight: people decide based on emotional drivers — being heard, being respected, being made to feel important — and rationalise afterward. The tactics range from the operational (use the person's name) to the structural (let them feel the idea is theirs).

**Kahneman (2011)** is a manual on how human judgement actually works. Its core insight: most decisions are made by System 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive) with System 2 (slow, deliberate, effortful) endorsing them — and the heuristics System 1 uses are predictable, exploitable, and frequently inaccurate.

Carnegie tells you *what to do* to land well with another person. Kahneman tells you *why it works*. They are complementary.

**Baby Bloom's aura discipline** is the third lens. It says: *all of Carnegie's principles + Kahneman's mechanisms are real, AND we apply them only in ways consistent with a brand that does not push, does not pull, does not perform, and does not over-explain.* The aura is the moral floor under both authors.

---

## The ten Baby Bloom presentation principles

These are what you actually do, distilled from the three sources and grounded in the brand's "never do" list.

### 1. Show the world they already want to live in

(Carnegie: arouse in the other person an eager want · let them feel the idea is theirs. Kahneman: System 1's coherence-building. Brand: the governing principle of content.)

We do not tell the parent her child should be ahead. We *depict* a world in which a child is being developmentally supported with care, structure, evidence. She brings her own ambition to the scene. She fills in the gap with her own desire for her child's future. The content is a mirror she pours her own meaning into.

The mistake to avoid: stating the dream outcome as a Baby Bloom claim. *"Your child will be ahead of her peers"* is both unenforceable (brand-banned outcome promise) and counter-productive (it places Baby Bloom in the salesman's chair, which collapses aura).

The right move: an image, a sentence, a moment that *evokes* the dream outcome without naming it. The parent does the rest.

### 2. Speak from post-struggle

(Brand: §The Feeling — "everything will be OK… not because Baby Bloom said so, but because the environment itself makes the worry feel unnecessary.")

We never reassure. Reassurance implies there was something to worry about, which keeps the worry in the room. We speak as if the problem has already been solved — calmly, with the certainty of someone for whom the path is simply clear.

Carnegie's equivalent: never begin by criticising or fighting; begin from the place where the answer already exists. Kahneman's equivalent: System 1 doesn't ask "is this true?" — it asks "does this feel right?" Post-struggle voice feels right because it doesn't argue.

Operational test: if a sentence sounds like it's *trying to convince*, rewrite it. If it sounds like it's *stating what's true*, keep it.

### 3. Let them bring their own meaning

(Brand: §The Aura Principle — "aura allows every person who encounters Baby Bloom to paint their own version of what this brand is, based on what they feel it should be." Carnegie: let them feel the idea is theirs.)

The corporate mum sees a brand that takes her child's potential as seriously as she does. The young nanny sees something that values her in a way no one else has. Neither is wrong. Both are true. Baby Bloom did not tell either of them what to see — it created the space.

This means we resist the urge to *define ourselves precisely*. The temptation is strong — Hormozi prescribes naming the dream outcome, Carnegie prescribes appealing to nobler motives, and both encourage explicit framing. Baby Bloom's discipline is the opposite: stay generous in the shape of the message so that the audience does the framing work. Less is more.

Operational test: would removing a piece of explanatory text make this stronger? Almost always, yes.

### 4. Specificity over generality

(Carnegie: use the person's name; remember details about them. Kahneman: vividness drives availability; specificity is more emotional than abstraction. Brand: the discipline of *named, dated, photographed* — every prompt that can use `{{child_first_name}}`, `{{nanny_first_name}}`, `{{specific_recent_observation}}` should.)

Generic copy is forgettable. *"Your child is developing well"* is air. *"Last Tuesday, {{child_first_name}}'s first independent steps at the park — {{nanny_first_name}} caught the photo within an hour"* is unforgettable.

This applies to every surface: pricing pages (the per-month effective rate at *this* child's age), trial-end emails (the specific milestones moved, the specific photos captured), payout receipts (the specific families this payment came from), Katie's acknowledgments (the specific work the nanny did this week).

The cost: substitution requires data, templating, real activity. The reward: every surface lands harder than a generic equivalent would have.

### 5. Honesty over manipulation

(Brand: never pushy / never pully / never manipulative. Kahneman: §4.10 — framing is the most pervasive tool in commercial communication; the ethics line is whether the framing OBSCURES information the user needs.)

Every claim Baby Bloom makes must be true. Every trial really is no-card-required. Every redistribution to the nanny really is half. Every cancellation really does stop the developmental work. Every refund really is handled by a human within 14 days.

This is partly an ethics argument and partly a long-game-strategy argument. Manipulative copy fails the moment users compare notes — and at Baby Bloom's scale, they compare notes constantly (in nanny WhatsApp groups, parent Facebook groups, on Reddit). A single instance of manipulation, once discovered, would cost more brand equity than the manipulation could ever extract.

Operational test: would the user, fully informed of how this surface was constructed, feel respected? If no, redesign.

### 6. Graceful exits + warm presence at every off-ramp

(Kahneman: peak-end rule — the last moment shapes the entire memory. Brand: never be needy; never escalate at goodbye.)

Cancellation, refund, account closure, trial-end-without-conversion — these are exits. They are also the most important brand moments, because the parent's memory of Baby Bloom is largely shaped by how she left.

The discipline:

- **Cancellation copy** names what stops, honestly. Offers a final summary as a gift. Says thank-you. (See [`PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md`](../PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md) §4.)
- **Refund handling** is human, ACL-compliant, real reasons + real alternatives on denial. (See [`PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md`](../PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md) §7.)
- **Account closure** is a two-step roundtrip with the safer option (cancel-subscription) surfaced; the destructive option requires explicit confirmation. (See [`PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md`](../PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md) §6.)
- **Trial-end-without-conversion** ends with a graceful goodbye, not chase. *"It's quiet without you. Just a note that your trial wrapped yesterday. {{child_first_name}}'s records are still there."* (See [`PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md`](../PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md) §3 T+1.)
- **Win-back** sequence is 3 touches over 90 days, then silence. The "promise — this is the last we'll reach out" line is part of the discipline.

A graceful exit produces returning customers and good word-of-mouth. An aggressive exit produces one-star reviews and warns the next prospect to be careful.

### 7. The aligned-incentive matrix is the moral floor

(Brand: the integrity of the structure depends on no party being the loser. From [`03-why-it-works.md`](./03-why-it-works.md).)

Anything Baby Bloom does must serve every party in the matrix. A surface that helps the parent but exploits the nanny breaks the model. A flow that monetises Baby Bloom at the expense of either user breaks the model. A tactic that wins short-term revenue at long-term brand cost breaks the model.

This is the test applied to every presentation decision. Hormozi-style aggressive checkout flows? Fail — they wear the parent down. Manufactured nanny competition? Fail — it puts nannies on the agency treadmill we're explicitly anti. Surge pricing? Fail — it breaks the trust the brand has spent months building.

Operational test: does this preserve every party's position in the matrix? If a single party is being squeezed, redesign.

### 8. Two registers — talk *about* them, talk *to* them

(This document is in the "talk about them" register. The surfaces a user sees are in the "talk to them" register. The two are fundamentally different.)

Internally (this folder, the WHY docs, sales scripts for the phone-onboarding team), we are frank. The parent's three layers of drive. The nanny's industry trauma. The deepest layer of FOMO. The notifications hitting five layers at once. These are operational truths. They stay inside the building.

Externally (the website, the app, the emails, the in-product Katie messages, the landing pages), we use the aura register. We never name what the parent or nanny is feeling. We never preach. We never reveal the machinery. The content is depicting, the language is post-struggle, the discipline is restraint.

The mistake to avoid: bleed. A landing page that reads "we know you want your child to be ahead" would be condescending. A landing page that *shows what early excellence looks like* does the same work, with respect. Same insight, two registers.

Operational test: if you can swap "internal" for "external" without rewriting, you are using the wrong register for one of the two surfaces.

### 9. Earned words only

(Brand: §What Baby Bloom Never Does — "Never speak more than necessary. If it can be said in five words, do not use ten. If it can be shown instead of said, show it. If it does not need to be said at all, do not say it.")

Words fill gaps. Baby Bloom does not have gaps.

Carnegie writes long. Hormozi writes long. Both have their reasons (Carnegie is a teacher; Hormozi is a salesman). Baby Bloom is neither. The brand's confidence is demonstrated through restraint, not through volume.

Operational test: read the copy. For each sentence, ask: *does this sentence change what the reader does or feels?* If no, delete it. Iterate. Keep deleting until every remaining sentence earns its place.

A common failure: explanatory paragraphs that defend pricing, justify decisions, or pre-empt objections. They reveal insecurity. The brand position is: we don't justify; we present, and the audience draws their conclusions.

### 10. Treat them as the sophisticated adults they are

(Carnegie: respect the other person; arouse nobler motives. Brand: §The Feeling — "the audience is sophisticated adults making high-stakes decisions about their children. Treat them accordingly.")

The parent is a corporate professional making one of the highest-stakes decisions of her life. The nanny is a working professional who has been undervalued by every system she's encountered. Neither should be patronised, gamified, or talked down to.

This rules out: emojis stuck onto serious copy, "mama bear" language, "you got this!" cheerleading, badges and leaderboards, infantile tone, FOMO emoji bursts, "Hey besties!" openings.

It rules in: clean direct prose, considered visual design, dignified naming (Katie, not "the bot"), specific acknowledgments, treating each person's time as if it matters.

Operational test: would a senior partner at a Sydney law firm feel respected by this copy? Would a UK working-holiday nanny who escaped daycare feel respected by it? If either no, redesign.

## The Carnegie translation table

Carnegie's principles in his 1936 voice, translated into Baby Bloom's operational register:

| Carnegie principle | Baby Bloom translation | Brand-safe? |
|---|---|---|
| Don't criticise, condemn, or complain | Never lecture parents. Never moralise. Never name competitors. Never reveal the machinery. | Yes — already a "never do" pattern. |
| Give honest, sincere appreciation | Katie surfaces specific work the nanny or parent has done. Unprompted. Specific. Real. | Yes — the structural appreciation lever. |
| Arouse in the other person an eager want | Show the world the parent already wants to live in. Let her feel the eager want she brought with her. | Yes — exactly the "let them bring their own meaning" principle. |
| Become genuinely interested in other people | The platform structure (Katie's memory, audience deep-dives, per-family detail) is institutional interest. | Yes — operational. |
| Smile | Cute. Not for this brand. The equivalent is *warmth through restraint* — Katie's voice does this without theatrics. | Translated. |
| Remember the person's name | The platform's templating system + Katie's memory render this structurally. Every email uses the name. Every Katie message references the child. | Yes — structural. |
| Be a good listener | Katie's listening is structural; the proactive system only fires when something is worth surfacing. | Yes — operational. |
| Talk in terms of the other person's interests | The audience deep dives + the aligned-incentive matrix are this principle, operationalised. | Yes — foundational. |
| Make the other person feel important | Specific acknowledgment, real recognition (especially of the nanny), per-family detail in payout receipts, "we'd love to have you" application moments. | Yes — repeatedly. |
| Avoid arguments | Never engage in competitor comparisons or defensive posture. Never argue pricing. Let the substance speak. | Yes — already a "never do". |
| Show respect for the other person's opinions; never say "You're wrong" | When a parent requests a refund, we respect it. When a nanny questions the commission timing, we explain the 14-day safeguard honestly. No condescension. | Yes — operational. |
| Begin in a friendly way | The "we'd love to have you on board" instant-acceptance moment. The graceful goodbye copy. The trial-offer "on us" tone. | Yes — already shipped. |
| Get them saying yes — yes — yes | NO. Carnegie's foot-in-the-door technique runs against the brand's "never pushy" rule. We don't engineer a yes-sequence. | Rejected. |
| Let the other person do most of the talking | The trial itself does this — let the product speak for 30 days; we listen. | Yes — structural. |
| Let the other person feel the idea is theirs | Same as principle 3 above. | Yes — foundational. |
| Try honestly to see things from their point of view | The audience deep dives are this. | Yes — foundational. |
| Be sympathetic | Translated to *dignified*. We do not pity. We do not condescend. We do treat the user's experience as valid. | Translated. |
| Appeal to nobler motives | The why itself (exponential education for humankind) is the noblest motive available. We let it operate without naming it loudly. | Yes — restrained. |
| Dramatise your ideas | NO. Dramatisation is theatre, which collapses aura. We show, we do not dramatise. | Rejected. |
| Throw down a challenge | NO. Carnegie's competitive framing breaks the brand's "never pushy" rule. | Rejected. |

Three Carnegie tactics rejected, the rest translated and applied. Carnegie's framework is largely consistent with the brand's discipline once you strip the early-20th-century salesman register.

## The Kahneman translation table

The principles already covered in [`20-offer-architecture.md`](./20-offer-architecture.md) and [`PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md`](../PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md), summarised here for presentation purposes:

| Kahneman principle | Where it operates in Baby Bloom | Ethics line |
|---|---|---|
| System 1 / System 2 | Aura attracts via System 1 (feel); honest math invites System 2 (decide). Both engaged deliberately. | Clean. |
| Cognitive ease / fluency | Smooth conversion path (low friction); the cancellation flow includes a "show what stops" moment that asks for System 2 — friction by design. | The asymmetry is honest; cancel is not theatrically frictionful. |
| WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is) | The trial pre-loaded with nanny activity ensures the parent SEES value before deciding. | Honest — value is real. |
| Anchoring | Upfront option presented first as high anchor; honest per-month math also shown. | Honest — math holds. |
| Availability heuristic | Specific recent observations in trial-end emails leverage availability; the items are real recent events. | Honest. |
| Loss aversion | Trial-end nudges name what STOPS, honestly. Nanny's earnings counter is loss-framed. | Honest — the loss is real. |
| Endowment effect | Trial period activates endowment via lived experience. | Honest — endowment from real use. |
| Peak-end rule | Cancellation grace, payout receipts, trial-end-without-conversion all engineered for the exit narrative. | Clean. |
| Mental accounting | Position the spend as "developmental investment" not "subscription". | Honest — investment frame is true. |
| Framing effects | "Pay once, done until they're 3" vs "annual lump sum". Honest framing of identical math. | Honest — both true. |
| Social proof | Nanny's in-person recommendation IS the social proof; we don't manufacture review widgets at scale we don't have. | Honest. |
| Authority | Founder credentials + the platform substance ARE the authority; no theatrical credentialing. | Honest. |
| Scarcity | REJECTED in manufactured form; only the genuine developmental-window scarcity is acknowledged, factually. | Honest. |
| Defaults | Auto-renew is opt-in via the subscription itself; no negative-option pattern. | Clean. |
| Hyperbolic discounting | Trial removes friction (no card); honest about what comes after. | Honest — no surprise auto-bills. |
| Status quo bias | Cancellation copy surfaces the safer option (cancel subscription, keep account) prominently when account closure is requested. | Pro-user. |
| Sunk cost | Three years of accumulated record IS sunk cost — but sunk cost from real value, not manufactured friction. | Clean — value-driven lock-in only. |

The pattern: Kahneman's principles are used *consistently* with the user's interest. Where they would conflict with the user's interest (manufactured scarcity, dark defaults, theatrical authority), the brand rejects the application.

## The "what we should present" question

The other half of Bailey's brief: *what we should present* (not just *how*).

Six categories of content / surface, each with discipline:

### 1. The product itself (the dev app, Katie, the platform)

Present through *use*, not through claims. The trial is the product presentation. Marketing pages and screenshots should show the actual product; case studies should be specific (with consent); demos should be the real product, not a mocked version.

### 2. The redistribution (what the parent's money supports)

Make visible. The receipt names where the money goes. The footer of the pricing page names the redistribution. The nanny's dashboard shows the per-family commission. Visibility is the lever; theatricalising it would collapse it.

### 3. The aura artefacts (the world the parent wants to live in)

Outbound social, Facebook ads, landing pages. Per [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md) §Content Principles: show, don't tell. The child as protagonist, the nanny as the practitioner, Baby Bloom as the quiet structural backdrop.

### 4. The honest math

Pricing page. Upfront-vs-monthly per-child-age math. The nanny commission timeline (14-day safeguard, when payouts hit, cycle structure). The trial mechanics. Per the brand: never over-explain, but never obscure.

### 5. The exit content

Trial-end goodbye, cancellation grace, refund handling, account closure. Per principle 6 above: graceful exits + warm presence. The brand asset is HOW they remember leaving.

### 6. The acknowledgment

Katie's specific praise. Payout receipts naming the work. Monthly summaries celebrating real moments. Per Carnegie's "give honest, sincere appreciation" and per the brand's discipline of specificity.

What we explicitly do NOT present:

- Outcome claims for the child.
- Direct competitor comparisons.
- Statistical guilt triggers ("children who…").
- Social proof at scale we don't have.
- Internal mechanics (status codes, conversion mechanics, commission math beyond what's already in receipts).
- The growth strategy itself.
- Aggressive multi-touch retargeting.

## The presentation review checklist

Before any consumer-facing surface ships:

- [ ] Does it pass the [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md) Checklist 1 (Brand Presence)?
- [ ] Does it pass Checklist 2 (Content Review)?
- [ ] If it's a product surface, does it pass Checklist 3 (Product & Platform Decisions)?
- [ ] Does it speak from post-struggle?
- [ ] Does it let the audience bring their own meaning?
- [ ] Is the specificity at its maximum?
- [ ] Is every word earning its place?
- [ ] Would a sophisticated adult feel respected by it?
- [ ] Does it serve every party in the aligned-incentive matrix?
- [ ] If this were the only Baby Bloom surface someone ever saw, would it make them feel that Baby Bloom operates on a different level?

If any check fails, redesign before shipping.
