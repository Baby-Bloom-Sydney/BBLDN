# 21 — Leads Architecture (Hormozi $100M Leads, through the aura)

> Alex Hormozi's *$100M Leads* framework applied to Baby Bloom. As with [`20-offer-architecture.md`](./20-offer-architecture.md), we take the underlying lead-generation principles and translate them through the brand discipline rather than adopting the surface tactics. The acquisition architecture Baby Bloom *already* runs (per [`WHY/03_THE_SYSTEM.md`](../../../WHY/03_THE_SYSTEM.md)) is unusually close to Hormozi's ideal — it just gets there from a different starting point.

---

## Hormozi's framework in one paragraph

A lead is someone who has signalled interest. A **qualified lead** is someone who could plausibly buy. The four ways to get leads: **warm outreach** (people who know you), **posting content** (people who follow you), **cold outreach** (people who don't know you), **paid ads** (people you pay to reach). Plus: **referrals**, **employees** (your team becomes a lead source), **agencies** (rented lead generation), **affiliates** (other people's audiences). The most valuable structure is one where **engaged leads create the next leads** — a flywheel where each customer becomes a lead source.

Hormozi's lead-magnet → core-offer pattern: give something so genuinely valuable for free that the recipient *gets value before any pitch*. Then offer the paid version. Reciprocity does the rest.

Baby Bloom's actual structure is almost a textbook implementation — accidentally, then deliberately.

---

## The four core lead-getters, mapped onto Baby Bloom

### Warm outreach — people who know you

For Baby Bloom: this is the **nanny ↔ parent in-person relationship**. The nanny is already in the family's home, already trusted, already in conversation with the parent about the child. When she recommends Baby Bloom, it is warm outreach at its most credible form — not as a sales channel but as a structural feature of her work.

Hormozi's prescription: warm outreach has the highest conversion rate but the lowest volume. Use it to test and refine the offer before scaling.

Baby Bloom's reality: warm outreach (nanny → parent) is the **primary conversion mechanism**. Every nanny who joins Baby Bloom is a potential warm-outreach channel to between 1 and 3 families. Per [`WHY/02_WHO_WE_SERVE.md`](../../../WHY/02_WHO_WE_SERVE.md), this is also the highest-credibility version of any sales motion — the parent sees results with her own child before the recommendation is even made.

**Refinement direction:** anything that makes the nanny's recommendation easier, more natural, or more accurate helps. The "Nanny pitching talking points" in [`PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md`](../PAYMENTS/COPY-AND-FRAMING.md) §11 are exactly this — not a script (which would sound rehearsed) but honest framing she can use in her own voice.

### Posting content — people who follow you

For Baby Bloom: this is the **outbound social on Facebook** (the primary channel per [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md) §Content Principles).

Hormozi's prescription: post valuable content consistently. Use lead magnets to convert viewers into leads.

Baby Bloom's adaptation:

- The brand's "Show the world the parent already wants to live in" governing principle (per [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md)) is the equivalent of "valuable content". We do not post sales material. We post *the world Baby Bloom families live in* — a child engaged in an activity, a developmental milestone being noticed, the warmth of a nanny + parent collaborating on a child's record.
- Lead magnets in Hormozi's sense (a free ebook, a checklist, a webinar) are **not the right form** for Baby Bloom. The lead magnet IS the matchmaking platform itself: free, fully-functional, high-quality. The parent gets real value (a verified nanny) before any paid-product ask.
- Hormozi's "value first, ask second" rhythm is literally Baby Bloom's acquisition architecture. We give matchmaking (real value), we give the developmental tools for 30 days (more real value), THEN we ask for the subscription.

The content discipline is documented in [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md) §Content Principles + §What Bad Content Looks Like. The Hormozi-style content sins — clickbait, trend-chasing, manufactured engagement bait — are explicit "never do" patterns. The aura governs.

### Cold outreach — people who don't know you

For Baby Bloom: this is the **job-site advertising for nannies** (Indeed, Seek, Facebook job groups).

Hormozi's prescription: cold outreach requires volume, precision, and a strong opening offer. Most cold outreach fails because the opening is generic.

Baby Bloom's adaptation:

- The job ads are precise (nanny positions in specific Sydney suburbs, specific age ranges, specific qualifications).
- The opening offer is implicit: *we're hiring nannies for great families* — the same offer Baby Bloom delivers. The ad itself is honest, not manufactured.
- Conversion path: ad → application → instant acceptance → AI-profile creation → viral share. The cold lead becomes a warm lead (a nanny on the platform) becomes a content lead (her profile shared back to her Facebook group reaches new audiences).

There is no cold-outreach component aimed at parents directly. Per the brand discipline, we do not cold-DM parents on Facebook, do not buy parent lists, do not run lookalike-audience cold outreach in a manner that violates the aura. Parents come through warm outreach (nanny → parent), content (Facebook), or referral (other parents).

### Paid ads — people you pay to reach

For Baby Bloom: limited and **brand-disciplined**. Facebook is the primary paid channel.

Hormozi's prescription: paid ads scale once warm + content channels are validated. ROI must be measured.

Baby Bloom's adaptation:

- We do not run aggressive performance-marketing campaigns that would dilute the brand. No quizzes-with-email-capture funnels, no "claim your free guide" landing pages, no retargeting that follows the parent for 60 days across the internet.
- Ads we run are **brand-respecting** — showing the world the parent wants to live in (per the content principles), routing to landing pages that maintain the aura.
- The platform's growth is structurally less ad-dependent than a typical SaaS because the nanny-as-affiliate flywheel does work paid ads would otherwise have to do.

The discipline is: **paid ads supplement the flywheel, they don't drive it**. If paid ads have to drive the business, something is wrong with the flywheel.

## The flywheel — Baby Bloom's actual lead engine

The full acquisition loop documented in [`WHY/03_THE_SYSTEM.md`](../../../WHY/03_THE_SYSTEM.md) §The Viral Loop, mapped onto Hormozi's lead-getter categories:

```
1. Nanny finds Baby Bloom         [Cold outreach: job ads on job sites]
                                  [Content: nanny profiles shared in Facebook groups]
2. Nanny applies                  [Conversion event: cold → warm]
3. Nanny is instantly accepted    [First moment of value: reciprocity activates]
4. Nanny does the reframe         [She asks what SHE wants; agency moment]
5. AI generates her profile       [Tangible value delivered: she has something polished]
6. Nanny shares profile to FB     [Content: her own audience becomes our reach]
7. Other nannies see, apply       [Cold → warm, compounding]
8. Parents in overlapping groups
   see profiles, discover BB      [Indirect: parent leads generated from nanny content]
9. Parents land on the site       [Conversion to authenticated user]
10. Matched nannies use the app
    daily                         [Warm outreach to the parent IS the daily product use]
11. Parent sees value, converts   [Subscription]
12. Converted parent tells other
    parents                       [Referral lead]
13. Retained nanny tells other
    nannies                       [Referral lead]
14. Loop compounds                [Network effects accumulate]
```

Hormozi's "more is better" intuition holds; *what gets more* is more important than *how much more*. Baby Bloom's flywheel generates leads in five of the eight Hormozi categories (warm, content, cold, paid, affiliate) and three of those (affiliate, content, warm) come from *existing customers using the product*. This is the structural moat.

---

## The lead magnet — free matchmaking

In Hormozi's framework, the lead magnet is the **free thing that's so valuable people would pay for it**. Baby Bloom's lead magnet is the matchmaking platform itself:

- Real, verified nannies. Real, structured parent applications. AI-generated profiles. Geographic precision. Matching logic that actually works.
- For parents: a high-quality alternative to chaotic Facebook groups + transactional agencies. Real value, no payment required, no card collected.
- For nannies: a curated platform that treats them professionally + opens distribution. Real value, no payment required.

This is enormously expensive to provide compared to a "5-page PDF" or "free webinar" lead magnet. That cost is the point. The lead magnet is so substantial that *it cannot be matched* by competitors using cheap-PDF lead-magnet tactics, and the recipient feels the difference immediately.

The paid offer (the development app subscription) sits on the other side of this gift. Hormozi's rhythm — give, give, give, ask — is the structural design.

## The nanny as affiliate — Baby Bloom's strongest acquisition mechanism

Hormozi treats affiliates as a separate lead-getter category. Baby Bloom's nannies are **the closest thing to a perfect affiliate program**:

| Attribute of a great affiliate | Baby Bloom's nanny |
|---|---|
| Audience trusts them | The parent has hired her; the trust is structural |
| Audience needs the product | The parent needs the developmental layer for the child the nanny cares for |
| Affiliate uses the product themselves | The nanny uses the app every day with the child |
| Affiliate's recommendation looks credible | Because the affiliate isn't selling — she is *demonstrating* |
| Affiliate is compensated for results | ~A$1,000 per converted family |
| Recommendation does not damage the affiliate's brand | It enhances it (she looks more professional) |
| Affiliate has natural distribution | She is in 1-3 households a week, plus her nanny social network |

A traditional affiliate program would pay 10-20% of the first sale to a marketing affiliate who has no existing relationship with the customer. Baby Bloom pays ~50% of the service fee to someone who has *daily, in-person, trusted access to the buyer*. The economics look different because the structural value is different.

This is why the commission can't be cheapened. The nanny's recommendation is the single most valuable acquisition mechanism Baby Bloom has, and the commission is what makes the alignment hold.

## The viral loop — the content-and-warm-outreach hybrid

The viral-share moment in the nanny onboarding (step 6 above) does two jobs at once:

1. **The nanny shares her profile to the Facebook group she found Baby Bloom in.** This is content posting (Baby Bloom's brand appears in front of new audiences). It is also warm outreach (the audience knows the nanny posting; her endorsement carries credibility).
2. **The share is intrinsically motivated.** The nanny shares because she is proud of how she looks AND because the share opens up ad-hoc babysitting opportunities for her. She is not asked to share for Baby Bloom's benefit; she shares because it's good for her.

This is structurally rare. Most "viral" loops require either an extrinsic incentive (refer-a-friend $10) or a manufactured trigger (share-to-unlock). The Baby Bloom viral loop runs on the nanny's own self-interest, which means it is **resistant to growth fatigue**. Nannies do not stop sharing because it stops being good for them — it remains good for them as long as ad-hoc babysitting is something they want.

The acquisition cost per new nanny is therefore lower than any paid channel could achieve, because the existing nannies do the acquisition work as part of their own value extraction from the platform.

---

## Referrals — the second-order lead source

Converted parents tell other parents. Retained nannies tell other nannies. This is the second-order layer of the flywheel.

Hormozi's prescription for referrals: build referral mechanics into the product. Make the share easy and the incentive clear.

Baby Bloom's adaptation:

- **Parent → parent referrals** are mostly organic in Phase 1. We do not currently have a "refer-a-friend" mechanism with explicit incentives. We probably should consider one — but only in a brand-respecting form (no "your friend gets $50, you get $50" theatre; perhaps a structural recognition like "your nanny becomes available for ad-hoc sitting for your referred friend's family" or "you receive a credit toward extending your subscription" — designs to be tested).
- **Nanny → nanny referrals** are mostly organic via the Facebook viral share + the in-person nanny community. Same considerations apply.

What we do NOT do: aggressive multi-level-marketing-style referral incentives. "Make money referring nannies!" frames the affiliate as a salesperson rather than a teammate, which breaks the structural model. Any referral mechanism we add must be quiet, dignified, and proportionate.

## The "Give Give Give Ask" rhythm — Baby Bloom's actual sequence

Hormozi's rhythm: give value 3-4 times before any ask. Each give builds reciprocity. The ask, when it comes, is small relative to the value delivered.

Baby Bloom's literal sequence from the parent's perspective:

| Step | What Baby Bloom gives | Cost to parent |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Free matchmaking with a verified nanny | A$0 |
| 2 | Welcome email + invite link from her nanny | A$0 |
| 3 | Full BB-app access for 30 days, including AI activity generation, Katie, full observation/diary/progress tools | A$0 |
| 4 | Daily content (notifications, observations, photos, milestones moving, Katie's surfacing patterns) | A$0 |
| 5 | A trial-end summary of everything captured | A$0 |
| 6 | The actual ask: subscribe to continue | A$200/mo or A$2,000 upfront |

By the time the ask comes, the parent has received approximately A$1,500-A$5,000 worth of value (matchmaking value alone is hard to quantify; a typical agency placement fee in Sydney is A$1,000-3,000) in exchange for nothing. Reciprocity is fully activated. The ask is a fraction of the value delivered.

This is the structural argument for why Baby Bloom does not have to push hard at the conversion moment. The pre-work has been done in the giving.

## What Hormozi gets right (and Baby Bloom should keep applying)

1. **The flywheel matters more than any single lead source.** Baby Bloom's nanny-as-affiliate + viral share + warm outreach + content layer all compound.
2. **A lead magnet has to be genuinely valuable.** Free matchmaking is the gift; cheaper lead magnets would attract weaker leads.
3. **First impression of the lead magnet sets the bar for everything.** The application flow's instant acceptance is the first moment; it establishes that this is not an agency.
4. **Distribution is structural.** Baby Bloom has built distribution into the product flow rather than treating it as a separate marketing function.
5. **Affiliates' alignment matters more than their commission rate.** The nanny's structural alignment (using the product daily with the customer) is what makes her credible.
6. **The ask should be small relative to value delivered.** A$200/month after A$1,500-5,000 of pre-paid value is the right shape.
7. **Compound rather than spend.** Baby Bloom's CAC trends down as the network grows because new nannies bring new audiences.

---

## What Hormozi gets wrong for Baby Bloom

1. **Volume-first thinking.** Hormozi often optimises for *more* leads. Baby Bloom optimises for *aligned* leads. A nanny in Sydney with three potential families is worth more than a nanny in any other geography Baby Bloom isn't currently in. The metric is not lead count; it is conversion rate × LTV × alignment.
2. **The lead magnet as marketing artefact.** Hormozi's lead magnets (PDFs, webinars, quiz funnels) are designed to be created cheaply and distributed at scale. Baby Bloom's lead magnet (free matchmaking) is operationally expensive and limited in scale by geography. This is correct for the brand. We do not need a thousand leads a day; we need the right hundred a month.
3. **Email list as primary asset.** Hormozi treats the email list as the company's core asset. Baby Bloom's core asset is the *network* (nannies + parents + their relationships) — much harder to monetise externally but much more defensible.
4. **Aggressive lead nurturing.** Hormozi prescribes long email sequences, retargeting, multi-touch follow-up. Baby Bloom does not pursue parents over 30 days of aggressive marketing after they leave; the brand discipline forbids it. The post-cancellation win-back sequence (3 touches over 90 days, then silence) is the right calibration for this brand.
5. **The conversion-rate-optimization mindset.** Hormozi often optimises individual funnels to extract every percentage point of conversion. Baby Bloom can — and should — do basic funnel optimisation, but never at the cost of brand. A 2% lower trial-to-paid rate is acceptable if the alternative requires brand-corrosive copy.

## The questions every lead-channel decision must answer

When considering a new lead source, a new ad campaign, a new referral mechanism:

1. **Does this respect the aura?** (Check against [`WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md`](../../../WHY/04_THE_BRAND.md) "never do" list.)
2. **Does it generate aligned leads?** (Parents in Sydney with 0–3 children + ability/willingness to pay.)
3. **Does it leverage or substitute for the flywheel?** (Leveraging is good; substituting is concerning — it implies the flywheel isn't working.)
4. **Does it preserve or damage the brand?** (Aggressive performance marketing damages; brand-respecting paid social leverages.)
5. **What's the LTV / CAC ratio honestly?** (Not lead volume; lifetime value over acquisition cost.)
6. **Is there an existing channel doing this less well that this could replace?** (Better to consolidate than proliferate.)

Pass all six and you have a Baby Bloom-style lead channel. Fail (1) or (4) and you have a Hormozi-style lead channel that will erode the brand.
